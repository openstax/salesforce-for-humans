Your Salesforce admin and data team are your partners, not your service desk. The quality of what you ask for directly determines the quality and speed of what you get back.

### The XY Problem

This is the single most common communication breakdown between Salesforce users and the people who build and maintain the platform.

**The pattern:** You want to accomplish X. You think solution Y will work. So you ask for Y. But Y might be the wrong approach, and nobody realizes it because you never mentioned X.

**Example:**
> ❌ "Can you add a picklist field to Account called 'Priority Level' with values High, Medium, and Low?"

> ✅ "I need a way to quickly identify which accounts in my territory are most likely to adopt Assignable next semester. I'm currently looking at adoption history, LMS type, and enrollment size to make that judgment. Can you help me build something that makes this faster?"

The second version tells your admin what you're *trying to accomplish*. The solution might be a picklist field. But it might also be a formula field that calculates priority automatically, a filtered list view, a report, or a dashboard component. By sharing the problem instead of prescribing the solution, you give your admin the freedom to build the *right* thing.

### How to Write a Good Request

A useful request includes:

1. **What you're trying to accomplish** (the business goal, not the technical solution)
2. **Who needs it** (just you? Your team? Everyone?)
3. **How often** (one-time analysis? Daily workflow?)
4. **What you've tried** (this helps your admin understand where the current tools fall short)

### Where to Find Answers Yourself

Before submitting a request, check whether the answer already exists:

- **Search existing reports and dashboards.** Someone may have already built what you need.
- **Check list view filters.** You might be able to get the data you need by adjusting a list view.
- **Look at Trailhead.** Salesforce's free learning platform (trailhead.salesforce.com) has bite-sized modules for common user tasks.
- **Ask a colleague.** The person sitting next to you might already know the trick you're looking for.

And when you do need to ask for help, the detail you provide upfront will directly reduce the back-and-forth required to get to a solution.
